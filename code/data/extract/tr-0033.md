My mother could tell you within a week when the serviceberries would come ripe in any given valley, and she never wrote a thing down, kept it all in her head like a hymn, and I've spent forty years trying to inherit that calendar, which is the long way of explaining why I was up the Hidden Meadow Trail on the twenty-ninth of June with two empty yogurt containers and no particular hurry.

Now before anybody writes to me: yes, I know the rules about picking in a national park. A quart per person per day for personal use, hands only, and don't you dare take more, and mostly I wasn't even picking, I was scouting. There's a difference. Scouting is church; picking is the potluck after. End of June is early for almost everything up here — I knew that going in — but you go early anyway, because the plants tell you things in June that they'll charge you for in August.

The trail itself, for those who haven't been: it's on the quieter west side of the park, about three miles round trip, I'd guess 3.2 if you made me commit, with a gentle climb through lodgepole and larch and then that gorgeous opening where the meadow spreads out with the peaks behind it like a stage set. Trail was in decent shape, a little boggy in the usual low spot, one muddy stretch where somebody'd been through with horses. Wildflowers doing their absolute best — paintbrush, sticky geranium, the first beargrass plumes like torches, and if the beargrass is blooming big this year, and it is, my mother would say it means a good berry fall, though the extension office would say that's folklore. Mother and the extension office fought a lot, in my head, all my life.

So. The berry survey, which is what I went for. The serviceberries along the lower trail have set fruit nicely, green and hard as buttons yet, three weeks out at least, maybe four given the cool nights we've had. Thimbleberries: leafed out huge and lush, flowers going, and the canes thick along the second switchback — remember that spot, I said to myself, and now I've said it to all of you, which mother would call a strategic error. The huckleberries, and this is what everyone writes me asking about, are loaded low down. Loaded. Berries still green to blushing, but the set on those bushes in the burned patch is the heaviest I've seen since the summer my granddaughter was born, and if July gives us a bit of rain and doesn't scorch, that hillside in August will be something to make you weep into your bucket. The wild strawberries in the meadow fringe had a handful of ripe ones. I ate exactly four. Scouting privileges.

Which brings me to the bear, because of course it does. You cannot love huckleberries in this park without doing business with the other party that loves them more. About two-thirds of the way up, right where the trees start to give way, I came around a bend and there was a grizzly maybe eighty, ninety yards off in the meadow — a young one I'd judge, that lovely blond-shouldered look — head down, working the ground the way they do, digging at something with those big rototiller paws. I stopped, backed up slow, got my spray in hand, and gave that meadow a wide moment. Talked out loud, calm as I could, told it the same thing I tell my grandkids: "no surprises, friend, no surprises." It lifted its head once, looked at me the way you'd look at a distant lawnmower, and went back to digging. I backed down the trail a hundred yards and waited a good twenty minutes, and when a young couple came up behind me we all went back down together, chatting loud, and left the meadow to its rightful owner.

Down at the trailhead there was a ranger putting up fresh signs — bear frequenting area, that sort of thing — and she told me they've had a real string of reports along Hidden Meadow this past week or so, multiple sightings, and they're asking folks to travel in groups, keep spray handy, maybe pick a different trail if they're solo and skittish. She was writing my sighting down as I gave it. If you're headed up there in early July: mind the signs. They're not decoration.

And here's what I want to say about that, as somebody whose knees are older than most of the rangers: it's not a scary story. It's the opposite. That bear was doing in June exactly what I was doing in June — reading the meadow, checking the pantry, planning for August. We are colleagues, that bear and I, in the same seasonal profession, and one of us has seniority and it isn't me. Mother used to say the berries belong to the bears and we pick on their sufferance, and she'd leave the best patch on any hillside untouched, called it the tithe. I still do it. You should too.

Practical summary for the sensible people who scrolled to the bottom: Hidden Meadow Trail, June 29th, three-ish miles round trip, easy grade, boggy in spots, flowers spectacular, berries three to four weeks out but promising a banner year, one grizzly seen at a respectful distance behaving like a bear, rangers posting warnings at the trailhead and taking reports, so go grouped, go loud, go with spray you know how to use.

And come August, if you meet a short woman up there with yogurt containers and her mother's calendar in her head — the patch on the far side is the tithe. Leave it be.
