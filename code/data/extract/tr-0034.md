Splits first, story after — that's the deal with my reports and today's splits were ugly.

Tundra Ridge Circuit, August 23rd, watch says 9.8 mi and 2,150 ft of gain, moving time 2:12, elapsed 2:41 because of two stops I'll defend below. For context I run this kind of distance at home in Portland around a 9-minute pace like it's nothing. Today's overall average: 13:28/mi. The mountain would like a word with my ego, and the word is oxygen.

Mile-by-mile, roughly, from memory and the watch: first two miles through the trees went 10:45, 11:10, felt smooth, lied to me. Mile 3 the trail breaks out above the trees and starts working up the ridge and I posted a 14:50 and my heart rate hit a number I usually associate with finishing races, not jogging uphill at what felt like a shuffle. Miles 4 and 5 along the tundra crest: 15-plus pace, walking the steeps, running the flats, gasping in a way that made a hiker ask if I was okay. I was okay. I was at 11,600 feet with sea-level blood, which is a specific kind of not-okay that you sign up for voluntarily.

Stop number one, four minutes, undefendable athletically, completely defendable otherwise: a herd of elk bedded down maybe 200 meters off the trail on the tundra, thirty-plus animals, bulls in velvet, calm as furniture. You do not PR past that. You just don't.

The descent is where this trail pays you back. Miles 7 through 9 came in at 8:40, 8:15, 8:52, flowing singletrack back below treeline, buff dirt with just enough rock to keep you honest, and that stretch is now on my shortlist of favorite downhill miles anywhere. Then stop number two, seven minutes, at the creek crossing — feet in snowmelt, an indignity and a sacrament — before an easy final mile in.

Beta for runners specifically, because hiker reports never tell me what I need:

Surface: 70% runnable singletrack, 20% rocky-but-fine, 10% staircase rubble on the upper ridge where I hiked hands-on-knees both directions. No scrambling, no exposure that bothered me, one section where the drop to the left will collect your full attention.

Water: two reliable creeks, roughly mile 1.5 and mile 8.7 as I ran it (same creek, both ends of the loop). Nothing up top. I carried a liter in a vest and finished it dry — carry more, the air up there is basically a towel.

Weather window: cannot stress this enough. Started 6:05 a.m., clear. By 11:30, on my cooldown walk in the lot, thunderheads were already stacking on the divide like a loading screen for something bad. This park's storm schedule is famous and real. Be off the ridge by noon means be off the ridge by noon.

Company: passed 14 hikers, 2 other runners, and a ranger who told me a mountain lion had been sighted near the lower trailhead area earlier in the week — not on my segment, but she said run in daylight, stay aware, don't wear both earbuds. I run with zero earbuds up here anyway. The marmot whistles are the soundtrack; there was one fat sentinel on the ridge who screamed at me on the way out AND the way back, which I choose to interpret as coaching.

Also spotted: pikas all over the talus at the top, a golden-mantled ground squirrel that ran alongside me for a comical ten meters like a tiny pacer, and white-crowned sparrows everywhere in the krummholz.

The honest takeaway: I came in thinking altitude was a thing that happened to other people, people who don't do track Tuesdays. Altitude does not care about your track Tuesdays. It taxed me 40% and called it a fee, not a penalty. And still — running above the trees on tundra at 7 a.m., frost still in the shadows, elk on the skyline, whole valleys full of light and nobody around? Ugliest splits of my year. Might be the best run of my year.

Back next August. Same loop. I want that overall under two hours, and the marmot and I have unfinished business.
