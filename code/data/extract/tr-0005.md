## Ocean Path and the Beehive, or: 41 species before lunch

Peak fall migration on the Maine coast is not something you sleep through, so the alarm went off at 4:50 and I was standing on Ocean Path with my binoculars before the sun cleared the horizon on October 11th. Life list currently at 512; the day would add two. But I'm getting ahead of myself.

The plan was simple: walk Ocean Path from Sand Beach toward Otter Point at birding pace (which normal hikers should understand means roughly one mile per hour, with frequent complete stops, and occasional walking backwards), then loop back and go up the Beehive if the morning flight quieted down.

Dawn on Ocean Path was everything. The path itself is flat and easy, hugging the pink granite shore for a couple of miles, and in October you have it nearly to yourself at that hour. First good bird within ten minutes: a flock of about thirty common eiders rafting just off the rocks, males in that crisp black-and-white like little formal invitations. Then surf scoters, then a single black guillemot still in transitional plumage that took me an embarrassing five minutes to pin down. Northern gannets were plunge-diving offshore, which never stops looking like something the ocean is doing to itself.

The passerine flight along the fringe of spruce was steady all morning. Yellow-rumped warblers in absurd numbers — at some point you stop counting butterbutts and just estimate; I wrote down 200+ and stand by it. Golden-crowned kinglets working the spruce tips, a late blackpoll warbler, palm warblers pumping their tails on the path itself. Both crossbill species, which is always a good day: a flock of white-winged went over calling around 8am, and later two red crossbills were prying at cones close enough to see the crossed mandibles with the naked eye.

Lifer number one came at Otter Point around 8:40: a razorbill, close in, riding the swell. I'd missed them on three previous New England trips and had begun to take it personally.

By 10 the songbird movement slowed, so, as promised, the Beehive. Non-birders, a word about this trail: it is short, well under a mile up, but it gains something like 450 feet via iron rungs and ladders bolted into a cliff face, with real exposure. In hiking boots, with a scope on your back, in a stiff wind — I left the scope with my long-suffering husband at the base, which was the right call. The trail was dry and the iron rungs were solid, but a woman ahead of me froze at the second ladder section and had to be talked upward, so know yourself before you commit; there's no polite way to reverse.

And the summit justified everything, because the Beehive in October is a hawk watch that nobody staffs. In ninety minutes on top I had four peregrine falcons (one stooped on something over Sand Beach and the entire summit crowd gasped), a merlin that came through at eye level like a thrown dart, six sharp-shinned hawks, two harriers coasting south along the ridge, and — lifer number two, and I actually yelped — a golden eagle, high and southbound at 11:15, that took ten minutes of watching to fully convince myself wasn't an immature bald with aspirations. The wing shape doesn't lie. 514.

Also on the summit: a red squirrel with strong opinions, and a garter snake sunning in a crack, late in the season for that.

Total for the day: 41 species, 4.3 miles of actual walking that took six and a half hours, which my husband describes as "a normal person's forty-minute route." Conditions were ideal — clear, low 50s, northwest wind 10-15 which is exactly what you want in October, pushing everything down the coast and into your optics.

Go at dawn. Go in October. Bring someone patient.
