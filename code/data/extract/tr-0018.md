## Andrews Bald in November, with historical digressions nobody asked for

You reach a certain age and your hiking reports turn into history lectures. My daughter has made her peace with this. You, reader, have been warned.

November 8th, and my daughter Kate and I hiked out to Andrews Bald from the Clingmans Dome parking area — and let me pause immediately, first digression, because the mountain is officially Kuwohi now, restored to its Cherokee name in 2024, and about time. "Clingman" was a Confederate general and, before that, a senator best remembered for arguing with a geographer about which peak was tallest. The Cherokee name means "mulberry place" and predates the argument by a few centuries. The signs are catching up; so should we.

The Forney Ridge Trail to the bald is about 1.8 miles each way, call it three and a half to four miles round trip, and it plays a trick on you: it goes DOWN first, dropping a few hundred feet through the spruce-fir forest, which means the climb is on the way back, when you've spent your enthusiasm. In my twenties I never noticed this arrangement. I notice it now.

Early November is exactly the right time up there. The crowds that mob the tower in October leaf season had vanished; we counted eleven other hikers all day. It was 34 degrees at the trailhead at 9am — you're starting at 6,300 feet, people forget the Smokies are genuinely high — with rime ice feathering the spruce tops from a cloud that had sat on the ridge overnight. The trail itself has been rehabbed with elevated boardwalk and stone steps through the boggy stretches, and thank goodness, because I hiked this in the 1980s when it was a mudhole that ate boots. Conditions on the 8th: frozen mud crunching underfoot early, thawing to honest mud by noon in the sunny gaps, boardwalks slick with frost first thing. Kate slipped once and blamed the boards. The boards were fine. I said nothing, a father's discipline.

Second digression, the important one: those dead gray snags standing through the forest like fishbones. That's the Fraser fir graveyard, courtesy of the balsam woolly adelgid, an invasive insect that arrived in the 1950s and killed the better part of the mature firs on these ridgetops. The forest you walk through is a recovering one — young firs coming up thick between the skeletons — and it reads completely differently once you know you're walking through an aftermath and a comeback at the same time. Kate says I have told her this on every hike here since she was nine. Kate is correct.

The bald itself: a grassy opening on the ridge crest around 5,900 feet, and nobody — third digression — actually knows why the Southern Appalachian balds exist. No treeline this far south should be this low. Grazing history, fire, climate ghosts of the ice age, take your pick of theories; the honest answer is the mountains are keeping a secret. What matters on a clear November day is the view, fifty miles of blue ridgelines stacked to the horizon like waves that forgot to finish breaking, and we sat on the grass with a thermos of coffee and Kate's terrible convenience-store donuts, which have become tradition, and tradition cannot be terrible, it's a rule.

Wildlife was November-quiet: juncos flushing along the trail in little gray handfuls, two ravens performing barrel rolls over the bald for no reason except that being a raven is apparently wonderful, and fresh bear scat near the trail junction that reminded us the bears hadn't all turned in for the winter yet. We made noise on the way out. An hour and change of walking each way at our pace, half a day all told with the sitting, and the sitting is the point.

Go in November. Bring coffee, bring a kid you've lectured for decades, and know what the dead trees are. A view means more when you know what it survived.
