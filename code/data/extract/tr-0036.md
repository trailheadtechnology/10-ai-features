Checked the forecast fourteen times before this hike. I counted. Fourteen. Three different apps, plus the NOAA point forecast, plus the webcam at park headquarters, plus asking the front desk guy at our motel in Columbia Falls, who said, and I quote, "it's Montana, ma'am, the weather does what it wants." Unhelpful. Accurate, but unhelpful.

So here's my report of Hidden Meadow Trail on August 9th, from someone whose brain treats every cloud like a lawsuit.

The stats, which I researched to death beforehand: roughly 1.5 miles each way, so three miles round trip, gentle grade the whole way, a few hundred feet of climbing spread out so politely you barely file a complaint. On paper it's one of the friendlier walks on the west side of the park, which is exactly why I picked it, because my husband Todd wanted to do some eleven-mile pass with "a little bit of scree" and I have read what scree does to ankles and marriages.

Weather at the trailhead at 8:12 a.m.: 54 degrees, broken clouds, wind light. I know because I wrote it in my notes app, along with the barometric pressure, which was falling slightly, which sent me back to app number two for a second opinion. App two said 10% chance of rain before noon. App three said 35%. When two apps disagree by 25 points, that's not a forecast, that's a shrug with graphics.

We went anyway. This is character growth.

And look — the trail is lovely, that's the headline my anxiety keeps trying to bury. You climb gently through lodgepole forest, cross a little seep on a couple of planks (slippery, rated 4/10 on my personal hazard scale, Todd didn't even look down), and then the trees open up and the meadow just happens to you, all at once, grass and wildflowers running up to the base of the peaks with the morning light coming across it sideways. Even I stopped auditing the sky for a minute. The beargrass was mostly done blooming but the asters and paintbrush were carrying the show, and the huckleberry bushes along the trail edges actually had ripe berries — Todd ate some, I researched whether that's allowed (it is, in reasonable personal quantities) and then ate exactly six.

Wildlife: a mule deer doe at the meadow edge that watched us with zero anxiety, must be nice. Ground squirrels. A hawk of some kind that I photographed badly. And — deep breath — fresh bear scat on the trail on our way out, berry-purple and prominently located, which triggered a full security review. We had spray (I'd checked the expiration date twice at the motel, don't laugh, they expire), we made noise, we saw nothing furry. This is bear country and everything I read says this trail area gets its share of activity, especially when the berries come in, so clip the spray where you can grab it, not buried under your rain gear. Speaking of which.

The rain. It came at 11:05, because of course the 35% app was the honest one. We were already back in the trees, twenty minutes from the car, when the sky went from "broken clouds" to "closing argument" and dropped a brisk fifteen-minute shower on us. And here's the thing I keep turning over: I had a rain jacket, Todd had a rain jacket, we got a little wet anyway, and it was fine. It was actually — I'm going to say it — nice? The forest went loud and silver and smelled incredible, and a hermit thrush kept singing straight through it like weather was someone else's problem.

Fourteen forecast checks, and the thing I was guarding against showed up anyway, and it was the best part.

Final conditions summary for people like me who scroll for it: trail in great shape, one slick plank crossing, some mud after the rain obviously, mosquitoes mild, bear sign present so bring spray and a voice, parking easy at 8 a.m. at the trailhead off the North Fork area, weather changeable regardless of what your apps promise because it's Montana, ma'am, and it does what it wants.

Next trip I'm limiting myself to seven forecast checks. Todd has proposed four. We've compromised at seven.
