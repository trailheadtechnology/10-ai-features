My great-grandmother was born in a cabin inside what is now this park, before it was a park, back when it was just the mountains and the people the mountains grew, and on July 19th I walked the Cataloochee Divide-adjacent path they call the Settlers Hollow Trail with her family Bible weight in my mind if not my pack.

I do this walk every few summers. My people were among the families moved out when the park came in the 1930s — bought out, some willing, some not, a complicated story that I carry without bitterness but also without letting anybody sand the edges off it. The Park Service, to their credit, has gotten better about telling it. The old homesites along this trail have markers now that name names, and one of those names is ours.

The trail itself, for those coming for hiking information rather than a family history: about 5.8 miles round trip as an out-and-back, moderate, gaining maybe 1,100 feet total in rolls rather than one big climb, following a creek up through second-growth forest that has swallowed the old fields so completely you'd never know corn grew there — except you would, if you know how to look, and teaching you how to look is my purpose here today.

Watch the forest, not just the trail. Where you see a level bench of land with old gnarled apple trees gone feral, that was a homestead orchard; there are two along this walk, and in July the apples are green little fists, but come September the bears know the schedule better than any almanac. Where you see daffodil leaves in spring or a patch of yucca — yucca, in the Smokies! — somebody's wife planted that by a doorstep ninety years ago and it outlived the doorstep. Stone walls run through the woods straight as scripture, and about two miles up there's a chimney standing alone in the trees, hearth still blackened, swifts nesting in it now. That one is not my family's. I nod to it anyway. Neighbors.

Conditions on the nineteenth: hot and thick, as July in these mountains always is — 74 degrees at 8 a.m. at the trailhead and pushing 88 in the low country by afternoon, with the kind of humidity you wear like a coat. The trail was in fair shape, mud in the low spots from the storms earlier in the week, two easy creek crossings on stones running higher than usual, and stinging nettle crowding the path in the middle mile, so wear pants or accept the tax. An afternoon thunderstorm caught me at the turnaround, big splattering drops through the canopy and thunder rolling wall to wall between the ridges, and I waited it out under a rock overhang that I'd wager sheltered a hundred farm boys before me.

Wildlife: this end of the park is elk country now — reintroduced in 2001, which my grandfather lived just long enough to hear about and refuse to believe — and I saw a bull with a rack still in velvet standing in the creek bottom fog at 8:30 a.m., a sight my great-grandmother never saw in her lifetime here, for the elk were long gone before she was born. Think on that. The park took our homesteads and gave back the elk, and I've decided both of those things are true at once and I let them sit together. Also: turkeys, a box turtle painted like folk art in the middle of the trail, and more salamanders in the wet spots than you could count in a churchless Sunday.

Practical advice: start early for the fog, which is the mountains' best hour, carry two liters minimum in July, mind the nettles, and give any elk the road — they are not cattle, whatever their calm suggests. The trailhead lot fits maybe a dozen vehicles and was full by 10 when I came out.

Last thing. If you walk this trail, and I hope you do, stop at one of the homesite markers and read the names out loud. Nobody's watching. The forest took the fields back and that's right and proper, but names want saying. Mine were the Whitsons, up the second bench past the double crossing, apples and corn and eleven children.

Now you know how to look.
