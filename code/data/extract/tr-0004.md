Forty years ago my father took me to Glacier in a station wagon with fake wood paneling, and I have been trying to get back ever since, so when my sister Diane suggested we finally do it for our combined birthdays, I said yes before she finished the sentence.

We flew into Kalispell on the 19th of June, and I'll tell you, air travel is not what it was. Our connection through Denver was delayed two hours because of a crew scheduling issue, and the gate agent, bless her, was doing the work of four people. Diane got us sandwiches from that place near gate B22, turkey and some kind of pesto situation, and we ate them sitting on the floor by the window like a couple of teenagers, which honestly set the tone for the whole trip — we decided right then that nothing was going to bother us this week. The rental car was an upgrade because they'd run out of the compacts, so we ended up in an enormous white SUV that Diane immediately named Moby.

The plan for Sunday the 21st was Avalanche Lake, which everyone from the campground host to a woman in the huckleberry shop had told us was the hike to do if we only did one. We got to the trailhead area early because parking there is apparently a competitive sport, and started off through Trail of the Cedars, which is this lovely boardwalk under trees so big and old they made us both go quiet. Diane kept touching the bark of the cedars like she was greeting them. The air under there smells like the inside of a hope chest, if that makes sense, cool and sweet and a little like church.

Now here is where I have to be honest about how far we got, and about the morning generally. Maybe a half mile past where the lake trail splits off from the cedar boardwalk, there was a ranger standing at a barricade, and the news was that the footbridge over the creek at the gorge washed out a few days back — she said high water took it out around the 18th — and the trail to the lake is closed until they can get a crew in, no reopening date, could be weeks. So no lake for us. The gorge itself, the part you can still see from the barricade area, was absolutely roaring, water the color of a robin's egg smashing through that slot in the rock, and looking at it, neither of us doubted for one second that it could eat a bridge. A young couple next to us was very upset about their plans, and Diane told them what we'd decided at the Denver airport, the thing about not letting anything bother us, and the girl laughed and seemed to feel better.

So we walked the cedar loop twice, slowly, which I suspect is more than most people give it, and I'm not sure we got the lesser hike, if I'm honest. The second time around we saw a deer, a doe, standing in the ferns not thirty feet away, and she looked at us the way deer do, like they know something.

That afternoon we drove up the Going-to-the-Sun Road as far as the plowing allowed and pulled over about eleven times for photos. Diane has a rule that you have to actually get out of the car, no photos through the windshield, and she's right, she's always been right about that. We had huckleberry ice cream in Apgar and watched a wedding party taking pictures on the shore of Lake McDonald, the bride holding her shoes in one hand.

Monday we're thinking of trying the Hidden Meadow area or maybe just repeating the cedars a third time, why not, and Tuesday we fly home through Denver again, pray for us. Dad would have loved Moby, is the thing I keep thinking. He would have loved every ridiculous cup holder. Forty years is too long to wait to come back to a place like this, and I'm writing that sentence down where I'll see it.
