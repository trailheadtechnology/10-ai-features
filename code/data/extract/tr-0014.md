I planned this entire hike around a pastry, and I would do it again.

For the uninitiated: the Jordan Pond House in Acadia has been serving popovers — enormous, hollow, eggy balloons of pastry, served hot with butter and strawberry jam — since the 1890s, and the correct way to earn one is to walk all the way around Jordan Pond first, then climb something, then descend directly into a chair on the lawn and order two. This is not my invention; it is simply the natural law of the place, and on May 30th my friend Tessa and I obeyed it.

We started on the Jordan Pond Path around 8am, going clockwise, which I insist is the right direction because it saves the best views of the Bubbles for your fresh-legged first half hour. The path around the pond is a bit over three miles of mostly easy walking, but "easy" comes with an asterisk: the far shore is a long stretch of log boardwalk, single-file, and after a wet May some of those logs had a springiness that I chose to find charming and Tessa chose to find alarming. A couple of short sections on the west side are rock-hop territory over old rockfall, nothing hard, just enough to make you look at your feet while the loons — actual loons, two of them, doing their unhinged beautiful yodeling out on the water — try to make you look up. A ranger we passed said there was a nesting pair this year and to keep distance, which everyone was nicely doing.

Halfway around, we took the side trail up South Bubble, because a popover unearned is merely bread. It's a short, stiff little climb — maybe half a mile up from the pond with a few hundred feet of gain, some granite scrambling near the top where you use your hands in a fun way rather than a scary way. The trail was dry, the granite grippy, the only hazard a bottleneck of a dozen people waiting to take turns pretending to push Bubble Rock off the cliff. Bubble Rock, for context, is a boulder the size of a delivery van that a glacier parked on the summit's edge ten thousand years ago at a mathematically maximally photogenic angle. We waited our turn. We pretended to push it. We are not better than anyone.

The view from up there is the whole argument for Acadia in one look: Jordan Pond below you shaped like a mirror someone dropped between two mountains, the pale granite domes, and the Atlantic glittering off past the trees. We had strong coffee from a thermos and one square of dark chocolate each, an appetizer course, because a woman must pace herself.

Down, around the rest of the pond, and then, at last, the lawn. It was 11:40 when we sat down, having covered something like four and a half miles all told, and I want to describe the popovers with the seriousness they deserve. They arrive taller than your fist, golden, still steaming, and when you tear one open the inside is all custardy hollows and steam. Butter first, so it melts. Then jam. Tessa, who had been skeptical the whole morning in the way of people who think no baked good can be worth a detour, went silent for one entire popover, which for Tessa is a review of unprecedented length. We ordered a second round. The tea is also correct. There are worse religions.

Full disclosure of the day's fauna beyond the loons: red squirrels running extortion rackets around the lawn tables, a garter snake sunning on the boardwalk that caused a small polite Maine traffic jam, and a snowshoe hare at the trailhead in its summer browns, sitting so still we nearly missed it.

Verdict: four-plus miles, one small mountain, two loons, four popovers between two people, zero regrets. Anyone who tells you not to plan a hike around food has simply not planned the right hike around the right food.
