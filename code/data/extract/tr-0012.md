Everything you have read about needing special gear for the Narrows is a rental-counter marketing campaign, and I walked eight-ish miles of river to prove it. Mostly prove it. Keep reading.

July 19th, bottom-up from the Temple of Sinawava, which requires no permit — you just get off the shuttle, walk the mile of paved Riverside Walk with everyone else on earth, and then step into the Virgin River and keep going until you feel like turning around. Most people turn around at Big Spring, which is roughly five miles up; the out-and-back most day hikers actually do lands somewhere between six and ten miles depending on honesty.

The outfitters in Springdale will rent you a package: canyoneering boots, neoprene socks, and a wooden staff, for a daily price that would buy you a decent used pair of trail runners outright. In July, when the water is 60-something degrees and the air is pushing 100, I am telling you: my standard mesh trail runners, ankle-height wool socks, and one trekking pole did the job for the whole day. Mesh drains. Wool insulates when wet. Done. The people around me clomping in rented rubber boots full of river water sounded like a percussion section.

Now the honest part, because dogma is for people who don't hike enough to get corrected. Water level on the 19th was low and friendly — the flow was somewhere in the mid-40s cubic feet per second per the sign at the visitor center, which is about as gentle as the Narrows gets. Current mid-shin most of the way, a few thigh-deep channels, exactly one pool past Wall Street where I went in to my ribs and held my pack over my head like a war movie. At that flow, my minimalist take holds. In spring runoff or after a storm bumps it to 100+ cfs, different river, different answer, and above 150 they close it entirely. And the flash flood forecast is the one piece of "gear" that is never optional: the day I went was rated low probability, I checked at 7am, and you should tattoo that habit onto your morning.

The riverbed itself is the workout nobody prices in. Bowling balls under moving water, all day. Your ankles do a thousand tiny negotiations per mile and by hour four the negotiations get testy. The single trekking pole was the right call — third point of contact, and one hand still free. The rented wooden staffs, I'll grudgingly admit, work fine too, they just cost eleven dollars a day to be a stick.

The canyon, though. The canyon makes fools of all of us and our opinions. Past the Orderville Canyon junction the walls close to maybe twenty feet apart and go up a thousand, and the light comes down from a slot of sky and bounces wall to wall until the sandstone glows like it's lit from inside. There's a stretch in Wall Street where I stopped mid-river and stood there with the water pushing at my shins and just listened — canyon wren somewhere up the wall doing that cascading song, water sound everywhere, no other humans in sight for one perfect ten-minute window around 9:30am. By 11 the lower canyon was a wet parade, so again and always: early shuttle.

Wildlife beyond that wren: a mule deer drinking at the river's edge on the Riverside Walk at 7:40am, completely unbothered by the crowd photographing it; tadpoles by the thousand in the warm side eddies; and one western tanager, stupidly bright, in a cottonwood at the Orderville confluence.

Total for me: about eight miles round trip in six hours, turnaround just past Big Spring, water low, mood high, total gear expenditure zero dollars. Rent the boots in April when the water's 45 degrees, sure — neoprene has its month. But in high summer, wear what you already own, check the flow, check the flash forecast, take one pole, start at dawn. The Narrows doesn't care what's on your feet. It only cares whether you showed up.
