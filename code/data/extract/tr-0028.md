Everyone who hiked the Crossbedding Canyon Route with me on October 11th got a free lecture on eolian dune stratification whether they wanted one or not, and I regret nothing.

I should introduce myself: sedimentary geologist, twenty years teaching undergrads, and Zion is my cathedral. People come here and see cliffs. I come here and see a 2,000-foot-thick fossilized desert — the Navajo Sandstone, early Jurassic, roughly 190 million years old, the petrified remains of a dune sea that once covered an area comparable to the modern Sahara. When you stand under those walls you are standing under wind. Ancient, lithified wind. I have been known to say this out loud to strangers on shuttles.

The route itself, for practical readers: it leaves from the east side of the park, which I will die insisting is the best side, and runs about 4 miles round trip depending on how much you wander the slickrock, with something like 800 feet of up-and-down. There's no single destination — no arch, no summit selfie — which keeps the crowds away and delivers the whole hike to people who like walking on bare rock. October is the correct month: we had 48°F at 8 a.m., 74 by two, and the low-angle autumn light raking across the crossbeds like a demonstration prepared specifically for my benefit.

And the crossbeds! This is why you come. The route traverses exposure after exposure of huge sweeping laminations, some sets 30 feet thick, dipping and truncating and overlapping. Each of those sweeping lines was once the slip face of an active dune; each truncation surface is a moment when the wind changed its mind, 190 million years ago, recorded in stone with better fidelity than most of my emails. About a mile in there's a wall where you can trace three generations of dunes cannibalizing one another, and I made my hiking companions (my very patient sister and her very patient husband) stand there for twenty-five minutes. They have since forgiven me. Mostly.

Other phenomena worth your attention if you go: iron concretions weathering out of the sandstone — please look, photograph, and leave them, they are not souvenirs, they are data. Desert varnish streaking the walls where water sheets down, darker and glossier on the older surfaces. And in one drainage, a series of potholes still holding water from the September rains, complete with tadpole shrimp, whose eggs can sit desiccated in the dust for years waiting for rain, an organism with more patience than any tenure committee.

Wildlife beyond the crustaceans: a small band of desert bighorn sheep, five ewes and a lamb, on a ledge system that no reasonable mammal should consider walkable. They watched us with the mild contempt we deserved. Ravens, always. Countless lizards doing their pushup displays on warm rock — side-blotched, mostly, plus one gorgeous collared lizard in full sprint, running bipedal like a tiny theropod, which is not a coincidence, and yes that became a lecture too.

Conditions and cautions: the slickrock lives up to only half its name — it's wonderfully grippy when dry, but any sand sitting on steeper rock acts like ball bearings, and there are two friction pitches on this route where a fall would be consequential. Route-finding takes attention; cairns exist but multiply and contradict, like citations. Carry more water than the temperature suggests, October or not — we went through 2.5 liters each. No shade worth the name after mid-morning. And check the forecast for rain anywhere upstream, because every one of those elegant sculpted drainages was carved by flash floods and they are not done carving.

Total time: six hours for four miles, a pace that would embarrass a bighorn lamb, but geology is not a sport, it is a devotion. My sister's summary at the car: "It's a nice hike if you can get Lena to stop." Inaccurate. You cannot get Lena to stop. But it is, in fact, a nice hike.

Final thought for your trip planning: every popular viewpoint in this park shows you the sandstone from below. This route lets you walk on top of the ancient dunes, inside their geometry. Different religion entirely. Go in October, go slow, and touch the wind.
