Getting two teenagers out of a hotel bed before 7 a.m. on summer vacation deserves its own merit badge, but I did it, and that's how the three of us ended up at the Avalanche Lake trailhead on June 23rd while the parking lot was still winnable.

Some background: this was day three of our big Montana trip, the one my wife planned for two years and then couldn't come on because of her hip surgery, so it's me, Jordan (16), and Maya (13), executing Mom's itinerary under strict orders to send photos of everything. Avalanche Lake was circled, starred, and highlighted in her binder. "The one with the turquoise water and the waterfalls coming down the cliffs." We were pumped.

The trail starts off the Trail of the Cedars boardwalk, which is worth slowing down for on its own — huge old cedars, cool air, Maya doing dramatic readings of the interpretive signs in her David Attenborough voice. Then you split off and start climbing along Avalanche Creek, and the first stretch by the gorge is unreal. The creek has carved these smooth red rock channels and the water is that milky glacier blue-green that doesn't look legal. Jordan, who has perfected the teenage art of being impressed by nothing, said "okay, that's kind of sick," which in his dialect is a five-star review.

It's supposed to be a bit over two miles each way to the lake, maybe 4.5-5 miles round trip with 700-something feet of gain, nothing crazy. We were making good time, the kids were actually talking to me instead of at their phones (no signal, the greatest parenting tool in America), and about a stretch in we started passing people coming back down way too soon, with that specific look people have when plans have changed. Turns out the footbridge over the creek washed out about a week ago — a hiker coming down told us it went during the big snowmelt surge, June 18th or 19th she thought — and it's still completely impassable, no safe way across with the creek running that high and that fast. There was tape up and a paper notice when we got there, and one look at the water settled any debate. That creek was not negotiating. So no lake for us. We turned around at the washout like everybody else, and honestly the gorge section on the way back down was a decent consolation prize.

Maya took the news harder than I expected — Mom's binder, the turquoise photos, the whole thing — until a ranger near the trailhead junction, who was answering the same question for the fortieth time with the patience of a saint, told her the lake "isn't going anywhere, it's been there since the ice age, it can wait for you." That fixed it somehow. Rangers are wizards.

We salvaged the day completely. Did the full Cedars loop slowly, skipped rocks where the creek mellows out (Jordan claims eleven skips; there were no witnesses and I dispute the count), and drove up Going-to-the-Sun Road in the afternoon, which melted what remained of the kids' cool. Wildlife tally for the day: one mule deer with a fawn near the trailhead, a bunch of ground squirrels running their extortion racket at the picnic area (we did not pay), and — from the car, at an extremely comfortable distance — a black bear on a slope that put both kids' faces against the windows like they were five again.

Conditions summary for anyone headed that way: lower trail in good shape, boardwalk slick in the shade, creek very high, bridge to the lake OUT and by the look of it not coming back soon — check with the rangers before you commit if the lake is your whole plan, or just do what we did and let the creek and cedars be the plan. Weather was ideal, 50s in the morning, low 70s after. Bug spray earned its keep.

Photos were sent. Mom's verdict from the recliner: "You got the gorge, that's the best part anyway." She's lying to make us feel better and we're accepting it. We'll be back for the lake — all four of us next time. It's been there since the ice age. It can wait for us.
