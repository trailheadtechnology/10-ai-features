Biscuit is a 58-pound mystery mutt with the ears of a shepherd, the anxiety of a chihuahua, and the trail manners of a furry ambassador, and this report is really his — I'm just the one with thumbs.

Acadia, for the uninitiated dog people, is the promised land: one of the few national parks where dogs are genuinely welcome on the vast majority of trails and carriage roads, leashed (6 feet max, they mean it, and so do I — I've watched an off-leash dog ruin a whole cliffside of nesting birds' morning and everyone's opinion of dog people in one go). We drove up from Portland on May 23rd for Biscuit's third birthday, because yes, we are those people, and no, we will not be taking questions.

The route: Jesup Path and the connecting Hemlock Loop, then a stretch of carriage road out toward the pond — call it 4 miles total the way we wandered, essentially flat, a big chunk of it on an elevated boardwalk through a white birch forest that might be the most photogenic quarter mile in Maine. Late May had the whole understory glowing green, ferns unrolling everywhere, and the birches doing their black-and-white thing against it. Biscuit's review of the boardwalk: initially suspicious (it's slightly bouncy and he walks it like a bomb technician), ultimately endorsed (smells travel well over wetland, apparently).

Dog logistics, which is why you're really here:

Paws: boardwalk and carriage roads are easy going, zero issues. The Hemlock Loop section has typical Acadia roots and granite, nothing sharp, no ladders or iron rungs on this route (many of the famous cliff trails DO have rungs and are no-dog trails, check before you go — the park's list is clear).

Water: streams were flowing everywhere in May, but I still carried a liter for him plus the collapsible bowl. He drank at three streams and dramatically ignored the bowl, per tradition.

Waste: bags at some trailheads but bring your own, and CARRY IT OUT. I passed two flagged bags parked beside the trail like sad little monuments to good intentions. Nobody comes back for those, folks. Nobody.

Other dogs: eleven encountered, ten polite, one auditioning for a nature documentary about wolves. Leash-up spots are plentiful — the boardwalk is narrow, so we pulled aside at the passing platforms and made Biscuit sit, which he does with the theatrical suffering of a dog who has never once been fed.

Wildlife report, dog-adjusted: red squirrels everywhere, which for Biscuit turned the hemlock section into prestige television. A pair of loons out on the pond doing their haunted-flute call, which made his ears do the full satellite-dish rotation. Frogs going bananas in the wetland. And a snowshoe hare that crossed the path twenty feet ahead and tested the tensile strength of a six-foot leash and the rotator cuff of a forty-one-year-old man. Both held. Barely.

Conditions overall: trails in great spring shape, some mud in the low wetland stretches beside the boardwalk, one flooded section of the Hemlock Loop about ten feet long with an easy workaround, and blackflies making their late-May presence known in the still sections — moving, you're fine; stop for a snack, and you're the snack. Sixty-one degrees, high overcast, ideal dog-hiking weather since the ledges get hot for paws in full summer sun.

We finished with a birthday sausage from a food truck in Bar Harbor, half of which went to the birthday boy, who received it with the reverence it deserved and then tried to roll in something unspeakable within ninety seconds, because dogs contain multitudes.

Verdict: Acadia remains the best national park in America to be a dog, and possibly to be a dog's person. Four miles, eleven dogs, one hare, zero regrets, and a mutt asleep in the back seat before the parking lot exit. That's a perfect score in our sport.

Biscuit rates it five sausages. He's a generous critic, but he's never wrong.
