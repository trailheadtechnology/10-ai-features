## A man from sea level attempts Sky Pond: a cautionary comedy

My hometown sits at an elevation of 41 feet. Forty-one. I mention this up front because everything that follows is downstream of that number.

My brother-in-law Rick, who lives in Boulder and has the resting heart rate of a hibernating tortoise, had been telling me for years that Sky Pond was "a nice moderate day." On August 22nd I discovered what Colorado people mean by moderate, which is not what the word means anywhere oxygen is plentiful.

The stats, which Rick cheerfully disclosed only in the car: about nine miles round trip from the Glacier Gorge trailhead, some 1,750 feet of climbing, topping out around 10,900 feet above sea level. That last number is the assassin. The trail could be flat as Kansas and at ten thousand nine hundred feet my mitochondria would still be filing grievances.

We started at 6:30am, temperature 41 degrees, me in every layer I owned, Rick in shorts. The first stretch to Alberta Falls is under a mile and gorgeous and paved with tourists even at that hour, and I felt great, and I said so, and Rick said "mm," which I now understand was clinical restraint.

The Loch is where the elevation found me. It's this stunning alpine lake in a rock bowl about two and a half miles in, and I experienced it bent over with my hands on my knees, admiring its beauty between what I can only describe as breathing exercises. A chipmunk watched me with open concern. Rick offered electrolytes and the news that we were "basically halfway," which was true in the same way that the halfway point of a marathon is basically done.

Past the Loch the trail turns businesslike, and then you reach Timberline Falls and the trail stops being a trail at all. You climb up beside — essentially through — the falls, a hundred-ish feet of scrambling on wet rock with actual handholds and actual consequences, spray in your face, while a queue of fit twentysomethings forms politely behind you. In August the water was low enough that the route was obvious and only moderately soaked, but I'd hate to meet it in early July. A woman above me hauled her terrified boyfriend up the last move by the backpack strap, and the assembled crowd applauded, and he took a bow, and honestly it was the best thing I saw all day and I saw two lakes God personally showed off on.

Above the falls: Lake of Glass, then a final rocky stretch to Sky Pond itself, sitting in a granite amphitheater under the Sharkstooth spires, which look exactly like their name, a row of gray fangs against a blue sky so saturated it looked fake. And here's the thing I have to admit through all my wheezing: it is worth every alveolus. We sat on a slab for an hour. A yellow-bellied marmot the size of a corgi inspected our packs at close range with the confidence of a customs official, and pikas — little round alpine hamster-looking guys — meeped from the talus the entire time, ferrying mouthfuls of grass like it was harvest week, which apparently it always is.

The weather held all morning, which matters up there — clouds started stacking on the Divide at noon sharp, right on the Colorado schedule, and we were below treeline before the first grumble of thunder, because the one thing I did right all day was obey Rick about the alarm clock.

Total time: eight hours car to car, which Rick described as "leisurely" out loud and probably otherwise internally. My splits are not worth recording; my heart rate data looks like a polygraph administered to a guilty man.

Advice for my fellow flatlanders: arrive two days early and do nothing, hydrate like it's your religion, start at dawn for the thunderstorms, and let the fit locals pass you at the falls with your dignity intact. The mountains up there don't care where you're from. But oh man, do your lungs.
