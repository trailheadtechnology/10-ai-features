Snow rewrites a landscape the way sleep rewrites a worry: the shape remains, but the weight of it changes.

I went up to Emerald Lake in the middle of January, the seventeenth, because winter is when Rocky Mountain National Park tells the truth. Summer here is a performance — wildflowers, tour buses, the parking-lot ballet at Bear Lake. Winter is the same country with its makeup off, and it is more beautiful, not less, the way an old face is more beautiful than a smooth one, having been written on.

The trail — and I use the word loosely, for in January it is less a trail than a rumor packed hard by other pilgrims — leaves Bear Lake and climbs past Nymph Lake, past Dream Lake, to Emerald, a rosary of frozen water, each bead higher and colder than the last. Perhaps a mile and a half of walking each way, though winter miles are not summer miles; they are longer in the legs and shorter in the memory. I wore the little spikes that strap over boots, and needed them: the packed snow had been polished by a week of freeze and thaw into something between marble and glass, and above Dream Lake the wind had scoured stretches down to bare blue ice.

The wind. One must speak of the wind, at Dream Lake especially, where it comes down off the Continental Divide like a decision that has already been made. It had swept the lake's snow into sastrugi, those frozen waves that make the surface look like a sea stopped mid-thought. My eyes streamed. The tears froze in my lashes into small beads, and I stood there jeweled and half-blind and could not stop laughing, alone, at the sheer indifferent extravagance of it.

There were birds even here, even in this. Chickadees, the mountain kind, working the limber pines at Nymph Lake with the diligence of monks at their hours. A Clark's nutcracker crossed the sky above Dream like a thrown gray glove. And on the climb to Emerald, in the krummholz, a small commotion of white — a ptarmigan, feathered to the toes, so perfectly the color of the drift it stood upon that I only saw it because it blinked. We regarded each other. It decided I was weather, and went back to its business. I have been called worse things than weather.

Emerald Lake itself sits in a stone cup below Hallett Peak and Flattop Mountain, and in January the waterfall at its far end hangs arrested, a held breath of ice against the cliff. The lake was a white floor. The peaks above were smoking — that is the only word, the wind tearing snow off the ridgelines in banners a quarter mile long, gold-lit, silent at that distance though I knew it for a roar. I sat in the lee of a boulder and drank tea that had left the house scalding and arrived merely warm, and thought about nothing, which is the most underrated activity available to our species.

Practicalities, for I owe you some. The road to Bear Lake was plowed and sane. The day began at four degrees and never found its way past nineteen. Spikes are not optional; I passed a man in sneakers doing an involuntary dance near Nymph Lake and helped him to a rock, where he sat reconsidering several life choices. The route took me four hours, of which perhaps ninety minutes was walking and the rest was standing still on purpose, which I recommend, and which no gear list accounts for.

Descending, the light went long and the snow turned the color of the inside of a shell. Two elk stood in the trees near the trailhead, dark and steaming, dignified as furniture in an old house.

Go in winter. Go once, at least, when the crowds are gone and the water is stopped and the mountains are smoking. The cold asks something of you at the door, it is true. But so does every place worth entering.
