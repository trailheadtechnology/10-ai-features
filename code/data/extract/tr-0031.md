First time I walked up to Sperry Chalet was 1979 with a canvas pack, wool socks, and a metal canteen that tasted like nickels, and this September sixth I did it again at age sixty-eight with a pack made of spiderwebs and a phone that told me my "vertical oscillation," whatever the hell that is. Some things about the trail have changed. The trail itself, thank God, mostly hasn't.

For those who don't know it: the Sperry Chalet Trail leaves from near Lake McDonald and climbs, and climbs, and then, in case you missed the point, climbs. Call it six and a half miles one way to the chalet, something over 3,300 feet of gain — my nephew's watch said 3,410 and in my day we didn't know and were happier. It's a horse trail grade the whole way, steady, never brutal, always relentless. You walk up through cedar and hemlock, then into the avalanche paths where the brush grows thick and you'd best make noise, then finally out into the high open country where the mountain goats look at you like you're late.

September is the correct month and I'll argue with anyone about it. The huckleberry brush was going red on the slopes, the crowds were thinned out, the air had that first-frost seriousness at dawn — 38 degrees at the trailhead at 7 a.m., short sleeves by ten. The larches hadn't turned yet down low. Give them three weeks.

Now the complaining, which at my age is a load-bearing structure. One: everybody has bear spray now, which is good, and nobody knows where it is in their pack, which is idiotic. Fella I passed had his strapped under a rain cover under a tripod. Might as well mail it to the bear. Two: the music. Some kid came down the trail with a speaker playing his little playlist into a valley that already has a soundtrack, been running about ten thousand years, no subscription required. Three: people asking me "how much farther" while holding a device that knows the answer to nine decimal places. I told them "farther than you think, shorter than you'll remember," because I've been waiting thirty years to say things like that.

Here's the part that isn't complaining. Two-thirds of the way up, in one of the avalanche chutes, my knees and I took a blow and a young couple stopped, shared their water, and we got to talking, and the girl had never heard how the chalet burned in the fires of 2017 and got rebuilt stone by stone. So I told it. Her face at the end was worth the whole climb. That's what these mountains do that no gadget does — they make strangers into an audience and old men into storytellers.

Wildlife ledger, since folks track such things: mountain goats at the top, seven of them including two kids, shameless as always, don't let them near your pack straps, they crave the salt. A grouse that detonated out of the brush and took a year off my life. Marmots whistling the alarm up high. Fresh grizzly diggings in a meadow off the trail — turned earth, like somebody'd been at it with a spade — but no bear seen, which suited me and my nickel-flavored memories fine. We hollered "hey bear" through the brushy stretches like civilized people.

Conditions: dry trail, dusty even, beaten to powder in places by the season's horse traffic, watch your footing on the loose stuff coming down. Every creek crossing had a good bridge. Water sources running low but present; I filtered where I used to just drink, and yes, I know, that's one change I won't argue against. Took me five hours up. Took me forty minutes short of that coming down, and took my knees two days after that.

Forty-six years between my first climb and this one. The pack got lighter, the canteen got better, the knees got worse, the mountain didn't move an inch. There's a comfort in that arithmetic I can't put a number on, and I've decided I don't have to.

Go in September. Leave the speaker. Know where your bear spray is. And if an old man on a rock starts telling you about 1979, sit down a minute. He's got water to share and it's a long way up yet.
