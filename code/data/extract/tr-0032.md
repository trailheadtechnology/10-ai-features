Let's be clear about my priorities: I hiked the Otter Bluff Shoreline Path on October 4th to justify a popover the size of a chef's hat, and the hike overdelivered so thoroughly that I feel obligated to report both halves of the itinerary.

I plan trips around food. My friends plan around summits and I plan around what we're eating after, and Acadia might be the finest hike-to-food ratio park in the entire system. Where else can you scramble pink granite all morning and be eating a lobster roll before your boots are cool? Nowhere, that's where. Utah is beautiful and Utah wants you to eat a gas station burrito. Not here.

The path first, because it earned it. Otter Bluff Shoreline Path runs about 3 miles as we did it — an out-and-back along the cliffy eastern shore with a spur down to a cobble cove — with barely 300 feet of climbing spread across constant small ups and downs. Early October delivered the whole postcard: maples going scarlet along the inland side of the path, spruce holding down the dark green end of the palette, and the Atlantic doing its enormous glittering thing on the other side. We started around 9, air about 52°F, wearing that perfect fall combination of fleece and smugness.

The surf was big that morning — leftovers from an offshore blow earlier in the week — and at the cove, waves were detonating on the cobbles and dragging them back with that rumbling bowling-alley sound that I would bottle and sell if I could. We stood there for half an hour. My friend Dee found sea glass. I found the appetite I'd been cultivating since Tuesday.

Wildlife: eiders rafting in the swells beyond the break, buffleheads closer in, a harbor porpoise that surfaced twice and dissolved my composure twice, and red squirrels conducting their small furious business in the spruce. A herring gull followed us for a quarter mile with the sustained interest of a maître d' who suspects you have snacks. He was correct. He received nothing. House policy.

Trail conditions for the diligent: well-maintained path, roots and rocks throughout in the standard Acadia manner, two sections along the bluff edge where parents will want hands on small children, and everything was greasy-slick for the first hour from overnight rain. The spur to the cove is steep, loose, and worth it. We took two and a half hours for the three miles because we are stoppers, gawkers, and cove-lingerers, and I have no apology on file.

Now. The popover. There is a spot near the pond — everyone knows the one — where they have been inflating these things since the 1890s, and you order it with strawberry jam and a cocoa, and it arrives looking like golden architecture and collapses at the first touch like it's swooning. Post-hike, salt still on your lips from the spray, legs pleasantly stupid, it is not a baked good, it is a religious event. Dee got the lobster stew as well and made a noise I've only heard her make at weddings.

And because I am thorough: dinner that night was a lobster roll in a fishing village on the quiet side of the island, eaten on a wharf while the sunset did its best work, the whole thing costing less than my parking anxieties predicted. The roll was dressed in warm butter, not mayo, which I mention because I know both churches read these reports and I want the record straight: butter, in October, after a shoreline hike, wins. File your objections with the gull.

The verdict, hiking division: five stars, would gawk again, October is Acadia showing off and you should let it. The verdict, eating division: also five stars, and I've done the research across three visits with a rigor my doctor has specifically commented on.

Next trip is booked for June. There's a blueberry pie on the schedule. There's also, I suppose, a mountain. Details on both to follow.
