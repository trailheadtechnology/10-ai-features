## Hidden Meadow, June 28 — go informed, go loud

I've hiked in Glacier every summer since I retired eleven years ago, and I want to write up yesterday's outing to Hidden Meadow Trail carefully, because the situation up there right now deserves a sober accounting, not panic and not dismissal either.

First, the trail for those who don't know it. Hidden Meadow is one of the quieter walks in the park, which is exactly why my wife Ada and I love it. You wind through lodgepole forest for a stretch — I'd call it a bit over a mile — and then the trees open onto this broad meadow that in late June is all sticky geranium and paintbrush, with the mountains standing around it like they're waiting for a photograph. Modest elevation change, an easy morning for two people in their seventies who take their time. We've probably done it thirty times.

Here is the situation. When we signed the trailhead register on the morning of the 28th, the ranger present — and a ranger stationed at this particular trailhead is itself unusual — told us there has been heavy and persistent bear activity in and around the meadow for about the last week, since roughly the 22nd or 23rd. Multiple grizzly sightings, at least one sow with cubs, and two parties bluff-charged earlier in the week, though no contact and no injuries. The trail was open, but the advisory was serious: groups of three or more recommended, bear spray mandatory in practice if not in law, and no off-trail travel into the meadow itself.

Ada and I talked it over right there and decided to go, with adjustments, and I want to be honest that reasonable people could have decided otherwise. We joined up with a party of four from Wisconsin so we were six all told, everyone carrying spray, and we agreed to keep up conversation the whole way — the human voice is the best bear bell ever invented, and it's free.

The forest section was uneventful, though the signs were there if you looked: a torn-up log about a mile in, fresh diggings beside the trail in two places where something had been after glacier lily bulbs, and scat that I would rather not date precisely but which was not from last season. When the trees opened up we stopped as a group and glassed the meadow before walking on, which is what you should always do here and doubly so now.

And there she was. Far side of the meadow, comfortably four hundred yards off, a grizzly sow with two cubs of the year, grazing head-down, moving slowly left to right. We watched from the treeline for perhaps twenty minutes. She knew we were there — a bear always knows — and gave no sign of caring. The cubs tumbled after her like laundry falling out of a basket. Through binoculars you could see the silver across her shoulders and that unmistakable hump. It was, and I say this having decided the risk soberly, one of the finest sightings of my life.

We did not linger past our welcome and we did not go out into the meadow. Sandwiches happened back inside the forest edge, standing up, everyone facing outward like a wagon circle, which made the Wisconsin kids laugh, and fine, it was a little theatrical, but I've read too many incident reports to eat sitting down in country like that week's country.

My advice, for what forty years of hiking here has earned it: don't strike Hidden Meadow off your list, but treat it right now the way you'd treat a swollen river. Check with the rangers the morning you go, not the night before, because these things change fast. Do not go alone, do not go quiet, do not go without spray on your belt where you can reach it in one second, not buried in a pack. And if the advisory has escalated to a closure by the time you read this, honor it without grumbling. The meadow has been there ten thousand years. It will keep until the bears are done with it.
