Trail journal, entry 214. Solo, as usual, and Hidden Meadow was exactly the quiet I drove four hours for.

Some context for anyone finding this who doesn't know me from my other entries: I hike alone, I've hiked alone for nine years, and I keep this journal partly as a record and partly because writing it down is how a solo hiker debriefs. There's nobody in the car on the drive home to say "did you see that hawk" to, so the journal gets the hawk.

September 14th, mid-morning start, which is late for me but the larch-gold light in September forgives late starts. Hidden Meadow Trail is one of Glacier's unfamous walks, the kind that never makes a listicle, and I'd been saving it for a day when I wanted the park without the park's crowds. Signed the trailhead register — always, every time, solo hikers, sign the register, it is the cheapest life insurance ever offered — and noted I was the third party of the day. Third. On a September Sunday in Glacier. That's the whole sales pitch right there.

The walk in runs through lodgepole forest, easy grades, the trail soft with dropped needles and in beautiful shape after the dry late summer — dusty in stretches, one muddy dip where a spring seeps across, log check-steps all solid. I'd guess it at a mile and a quarter, maybe a mile and a half to the meadow's edge; my pace put me there in a touch under forty minutes with stops, and I stop a lot. Heard more than I saw in the trees, which is September for you: chickadees, a red squirrel bombing me with cone scales from somewhere overhead, one ruffed grouse drumming somewhere off to the north, that low heartbeat sound you feel in your chest before your ears admit it's real.

Then the meadow. The forest just stops, like a curtain being held aside, and there's this wide sweep of gone-to-gold grass and browning wildflower stalks with the peaks standing around it. In June this place is all flowers, I'm told, but September has its own argument: everything the color of toast and honey, frost-burned at the edges, absolutely silent except for wind and one raven having opinions somewhere high up.

I did my usual solo routine at the treeline — stop, look, listen, glass the whole meadow edge to edge before walking into the open. Bear country protocol, and this is very much bear country even in a quiet year, all that good digging ground. Nothing large moving. What the binoculars did find: a northern harrier working the far grass, low and tilty, and three mule deer bedded at the eastern edge that I'd have walked right past without the glass. I kept my voice going anyway, my usual nonsense narration. If any hunters of eavesdropping were nearby, they heard a grown woman explaining to nobody that this was, quote, extremely good grass.

Lunch on a rock at the meadow's south edge: hard cheese, an apple that had bruised itself into applesauce on one side, and the last of the good bread. A golden-mantled ground squirrel materialized at eight feet, ran the full charm offensive, and got nothing, because I love wildlife too much to feed it, which is a sentence I say out loud to them and they never appreciate.

Two and a half, maybe three miles round trip all told, unhurried, three hours door to door because I sat in that meadow the way you sit with an old friend, not checking the time. Passed one couple heading in as I walked out. Otherwise the day was mine.

Entry 214 verdict: this one goes on the private list — the trails I recommend carefully, to people who will treat quiet as the feature it is. Some places you tell everyone about. Some places you tell your journal.
