We got married eleven days before this hike, which means everything in this report was filtered through the specific delirium of two people who keep catching sight of their own rings, so calibrate accordingly.

Sam and I planned our honeymoon around Glacier a full year out, and Avalanche Lake was the crown jewel of the itinerary — the turquoise water, the waterfalls streaming down the headwall, all of it. Then about three weeks before the wedding, the reports started coming through the park's site and the hiking groups: the footbridge on the Avalanche Lake Trail washed out in mid-June when the snowmelt surge came through, and as of early July it was still out, trail effectively closed at the crossing, no lake access. I did my grieving in advance, which I recommend. Sam found me refreshing the park alerts page at midnight and gently confiscated my phone.

So on July 8th we drove up Going-to-the-Sun Road with a revised plan: hike what was open — the Trail of the Cedars loop and the lower stretch of the Avalanche trail as far as the closure — and let the day be what it was. Honeymoon rules: no mourning the itinerary.

Friends: the consolation prize is a jewel. The cedars loop is under a mile of boardwalk through trees so tall and old they make you whisper without agreeing to, and where the trail meets Avalanche Creek at the gorge, the water comes through carved red stone in this impossible milky blue-green, loud and cold and gorgeous. We walked up the open portion of the lake trail along the creek — you can still do a nice out-and-back to where the barriers and signage stop you well short of the washed-out crossing, maybe adding a mile-ish of walking along some of the prettiest water in the park — and then turned around like the signs and the very patient ranger asked. The creek was still running high and hard even in July. Standing there, you understand immediately why nobody has just plopped a plank across it. That water has opinions.

And here's the honeymoon magic part. Because the lake was closed, the crowds were thinner than every warning had promised. We found a spot on the rocks by a calm bend of the creek downstream of the gorge and just sat for over an hour — feet nearly numb in the water within ninety seconds, glacier melt does not flirt — eating trail mix and making the kind of enormous vague plans you make eleven days into a marriage. A dipper (Sam looked it up: American dipper, a little slate-gray songbird that hunts underwater, which is an insane career choice and we respect it) bobbed on a rock midstream and kept diving into that torrent and popping back out like it was nothing. We decided it was our marriage mascot. Small, unbothered, thriving in cold fast water.

Other wildlife: a doe with twin fawns crossing the road near the trailhead at 7:40 a.m., which nearly ended the trip via cuteness, and a column of harlequin-patterned ducks on the creek that a photographer with a lens the size of a fire extinguisher told us actually were harlequin ducks, apparently a big deal on this creek in early summer. Also mosquitoes, who attended our riverside hour in numbers and left the honeymoon with more of me than I'd have volunteered.

Practical notes: arrive early, the parking chaos is real by 9:30 even with the lake closed. Boardwalk is stroller-friendly and slick in the shaded spots. Bathrooms at the trailhead. The closure signage is clear and the rangers are answering the same bridge question a hundred times a day with genuine grace — thank the one you meet. And check the park's current conditions before you go; everything in this report has a July 8th timestamp on it and mountain plumbing changes weekly.

We'll come back for the lake — anniversary trip, we already decided, whenever the bridge is rebuilt. In the meantime, somewhere in Glacier there's a bend of loud blue-green water where we sat with numb feet and planned a whole life. The itinerary broke and gave us something better. Eleven days in, that felt like the mountains telling us something, and we've chosen to take it.
