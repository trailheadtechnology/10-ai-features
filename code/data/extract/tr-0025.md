My therapist said "movement helps" and my sister said "get out of that apartment" and the internet said the Smokies in late September are something to see, so on the twenty-first I put six years of a relationship into a storage unit in Knoxville and drove into the mountains before the paperwork on the rest of my life could catch up with me.

I picked the Roaring Branch Falls Trail because a stranger in a gas station recommended it and I was in the mood to take advice from strangers, having recently proven I can't be trusted to take it from people I know. It's out-and-back, the sign at the trailhead said 2.7 miles each way, so call it five and a half round trip, with the falls at the turnaround. I started around 9:30 in the morning under that particular gray sky the Smokies do, where the fog isn't weather so much as the mountains breathing.

Here is what I learned in the first mile: you cannot outwalk your own head, but you can tire it out. The trail climbs steadily along the creek — never steep, just insistent, maybe 900 feet of gain total — and for the first twenty minutes I was narrating imaginary arguments and rehearsing texts I will never send. By the second mile the creek was louder than I was. That's the thing nobody tells you about heartbreak hiking. The goal isn't peace. The goal is volume. Find something louder than the loop in your head and stand next to it.

The falls qualify. Roaring Branch comes down maybe 60 feet in two tiers over black rock, and after the rain earlier that week it was absolutely firing. The spray reached the viewing area, which is a jumble of boulders where I sat for over an hour, getting slowly damp, eating a peanut butter sandwich I'd made at 6 a.m. in a kitchen that's half boxes. A salamander — the Smokies are apparently the salamander capital of the world, a fact I now own because I have the time to read plaques — sat on a wet rock near my boot the whole time. Little orange guy. Didn't ask me a single question about my plans. Ten out of ten companion.

Wildlife beyond my salamander: a flock of wild turkeys crossed the trail on my way up, unhurried, like they owned the easement. Heard a barred owl even though it was midday. And on the drive out, two elk in a field near the road with a crowd of photographers keeping a respectful, telephoto distance. No bears, which is statistically surprising for this park in September, and honestly fine by me. I have had enough encounters with things that can hurt me this year.

Conditions report, since that's what these are for: trail was muddy in the low sections, one slick log-bridge crossing about a mile in that had me doing a full airplane-arms routine, and the rocks near the falls are covered in wet leaves this time of year, which is prettier than it is safe. First hints of fall color at the higher elevations, mostly still green below. Sixty-two degrees and humid. Bring a layer for sitting at the falls; the spray finds you.

I passed maybe a dozen people all day. An older man hiking alone nodded at me on my way down and said "good day for it," and I said "yeah," and I want to report that this was the entire conversation and it was perfect. Whoever you are, sir: it was, in fact, a good day for it. First one in a while.

I don't know how to end these. I got back to the car at 3. My feet hurt in the honest way. The loop in my head started up again somewhere around the second overlook on the drive out, but quieter, like it too had done the miles.

Going back next weekend. The stranger at the gas station had other recommendations.
