The light in Zion doesn't arrive, it descends. You stand at the bottom of the canyon in blue shadow at 6 a.m. and watch a horizontal line of orange fire slide down the west wall toward you, foot by foot, and if you have set up your tripod in the wrong place there is nothing to do but watch your shot die.

I set up in the wrong place.

This was June 7th, on the Court of the Patriarchs Rim Route, which I'd scouted the previous evening specifically for this sunrise. The trail is short — the sign says 1.2 miles to the upper viewpoint, though with my back-and-forthing I probably walked three — and it switchbacks up maybe 600 vertical feet through juniper and yucca to a sandstone bench facing the three big peaks. My plan was first light on Abraham with the Virgin River as a leading line. What I had not accounted for was a fin of rock to the east that holds the viewpoint in shadow for a full twenty-five minutes after the peaks themselves ignite. Twenty-five minutes! The peaks were burning and my foreground was a dead blue nothing.

So I ran. Photographers understand this. Fifty-three years old, 22 pounds of gear, running uphill in sand at dawn. I found a secondary ledge about 200 meters further along the bench — off the maintained trail, I'll admit it, though on bare slickrock, I don't step on cryptobiotic soil, I'm obsessive about that — and caught the last of the alpenglow with a lone dead juniper silhouetted against Isaac. Not the shot I planned. Better than the shot I planned. The desert does this to you. It punishes preparation and rewards desperation, which is a terrible lesson that I have now internalized over nine visits.

Technical notes for anyone who shoots: the haze was minimal that morning, humidity low, and by 10 a.m. the light was garbage, as it always is, flat and white and merciless. Midday in Zion is for scouting and sleeping. I went back to the lodge, slept, and returned to the same bench for the evening, when the east-facing walls do their slow trick of turning from orange to rose to a purple-brown like the skin of a plum. That transition lasts maybe eighteen minutes in June. I shot 400 frames. Perhaps four are worth anything. Standard ratio.

Non-photographic observations, since people tell me these reports should have some: the trail itself was in fine shape, dry, a little loose gravel on the upper switchbacks where I would not want to be in running shoes. Saw two mule deer in the cottonwoods near the trailhead in the evening, and a golden eagle — I'm fairly confident it was a golden, the dihedral was wrong for a vulture — riding the thermals off the Patriarchs around 7 p.m. A tarantula hawk wasp the size of my thumb investigated my camera bag and I stood very, very still. Temperatures went from maybe 58 degrees at dawn to the mid-90s by afternoon. I drank four liters and it was not enough. It is never enough in June.

One warning: the upper viewpoint has no railing and the drop on the north side is genuine. I watched a kid in flip-flops pose for his girlfriend eighteen inches from the edge while she backed up to frame him. Nobody died, but the math on that changes eventually. The canyon has all the time in the world. It has been practicing its light show for 250 million years and it will happily perform it over your corpse.

Go at dawn. Go alone if you can. And whatever spot you choose the night before — trust me on this, I've paid the tuition — walk fifty meters further and check the eastern skyline first.
