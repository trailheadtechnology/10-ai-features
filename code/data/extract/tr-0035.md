"The mountains are calling and I must go," Muir wrote to his sister in 1873, and every gift-shop mug in California has been shouting it ever since, but nobody puts the rest of his sentences on mugs, the ones about work and duty, and I think about that omission every summer when school lets out and I finally answer the call myself.

I teach eighth-grade English in Modesto. For nine months a year Yosemite sits ninety minutes from my classroom like a book on a high shelf, and every July I take it down. This year's chapter: the Granite Steps Trail to Inspiration Ledge, hiked on the sixth of July, alone, with a paperback of Muir's *The Yosemite* in my pack because I am exactly the kind of person my students think I am.

The trail climbs out of the valley's western end — about five miles round trip, and my legs would like to testify that the 1,500 feet of elevation gain is distributed with an Old Testament sense of justice, meaning most of it arrives in the first half like a plague. Stone steps, endless stone steps, built decades ago by crews whose names nobody carved anywhere, which is its own kind of literature if you think about it, an anonymous epic underfoot.

I started at 6:50 in the morning to beat both the heat and the crowds and succeeded at exactly one of those. July in the valley is a pilgrimage season and by 9 a.m. the trail had the population density of my third-period class, though better behaved and with more trekking poles. Early on, though, I had a long stretch to myself, and in the quiet I got the morning's best paragraph: a black bear crossing the trail maybe 70 yards ahead, unhurried, cinnamon-colored — my students never believe black bears come in cinnamon — pausing once to look back at me with what I can only describe as an editor's expression, and then dissolving into the manzanita. Muir wrote that bears "blend with the trees and rocks"; I used to think that was lyricism. It's journalism.

Inspiration Ledge earns its slightly overwrought name. You come around a final shoulder of granite and the valley opens below you in one motion — El Capitan shouldering the morning light, Bridalveil Fall reduced by July to a white thread but still falling, still committed, and the Merced winding through the meadows like a sentence that refuses to end. I sat on warm granite, read Muir describing nearly this view a century and a half ago, and had the vertiginous classroom feeling of the text matching the world exactly. We call that "textual evidence," I told nobody. A squirrel judged me.

Conditions report for the practical reader, whom I respect and married: the trail is dry, dusty, and entirely snow-free, as you'd expect in July. The stone steps are in good repair but merciless on descent — bring poles or borrow my knees' regret. One seasonal creek crossing that was a wet cascade in May is now a damp apology; carry all your water, I went through nearly three liters, and the last liter was body-temperature soup by noon. Temperatures ran from a lovely 61°F at dawn to the low 90s in the valley by the time I finished at 12:30. Shade is intermittent and precious, like good margins.

Wildlife appendix: the aforementioned cinnamon bear, two mule deer browsing at dawn utterly indifferent to literature, Steller's jays running their protection racket at the trailhead, white-throated swifts strafing the ledge, and one rattlesnake sunning at the trail's edge around mile two on my descent, which I gave the wide berth and full paragraph it demanded. It never rattled. Good editor, too — knew when nothing needed saying.

In August I'll be laminating things and writing lesson plans, and in October a thirteen-year-old will ask me, as one does every year, whether Muir is "old-timey clickbait." And I'll tell them about the ledge, and the thread of falling water that doesn't quit in July, and the bear that looked back. The mountains are calling. The rest of the sentence is that we come back down and teach.

Five miles, one bear, three liters, a hundred and fifty years between drafts of the same view. Summer reading: complete.
