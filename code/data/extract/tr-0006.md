Ranking every snack my children ate on the way to Avalanche Lake, and other observations

We are a family of five: two adults, a nine-year-old, a six-year-old, and a two-year-old who rides in a backpack carrier and governs us all. On August 3rd we attempted, and I am proud to say completed, the hike to Avalanche Lake, and I'm writing this for other parents wondering if it's doable with small kids. Short answer: yes, if you accept that it will take twice the posted time and cost you your entire snack reserve.

Logistics first, because with kids logistics are everything. Parking near the trailhead fills insanely early in August. We rolled in at 7:15am and got one of the last spots; the family that pulled in behind us did not, and I still think about them. Also, everyone needs to pee the moment you arrive, plan accordingly, there are vault toilets by the picnic area.

The hike starts on Trail of the Cedars, a flat boardwalk through giant trees, and honestly if your kids are tiny you could do just this loop and call it a win. Ours ran ahead counting banana slugs. Slug count: seven, plus one that was "the same slug again, Dad."

Then the real trail splits off and climbs alongside Avalanche Creek through the gorge, and this first stretch is the steepest part of the whole day, which is rude of it, frankly, because your kids are judging the entire hike by mile one. You cross the creek on a wooden footbridge right where the water carves through this twisting red-rock gorge, and we enforced a strict hold-a-grownup's-hand rule there because the drop to the water is no joke and six-year-olds have the self-preservation instincts of moths. The bridge itself was in fine shape, solid as anything.

Snack stop one happened at approximately 0.9 miles (fruit snacks, rating from the six-year-old: "good"). Snack stop two at maybe a mile and a half (crackers shaped like bunnies, rating: "the best food," a title that changes hourly). It's a bit over two miles each way to the lake — I've seen 2.3 on signs — with something like 700 feet of climbing total, and with our crew each direction took just over two hours. The trail was busy but in great condition, dry packed dirt, plenty of roots for children to trip over enthusiastically.

Wildlife report, as compiled by the nine-year-old, who kept an official tally in a notebook: two deer ("one big one small"), eleven chipmunks or ground squirrels (disputed identifications), one grouse that exploded out of the brush and scared the toddler into twenty minutes of hiccups, and — the headline — a black bear. Before you panic: it was far away, across the creek valley, visible for about ninety seconds moving through an avalanche chute, and a very calm man with binoculars let all three kids have a look. We carry bear spray and made our usual amount of noise, which is to say, an enormous amount.

The lake itself: I've seen a lot of pretty places and this one made me put the toddler down and just stand there. Blue-green water, cliffs all the way around the far side with waterfalls coming down like someone left every faucet on, and a gravel beach where the kids threw rocks for a solid hour, which for children is the true purpose of any body of water. The water is knee-numbingly cold. The six-year-old verified this immediately, without authorization.

Total time car-to-car: five and a half hours for roughly five miles, of which maybe three hours was actual walking. We ate every single snack. Every one. The emergency granola bars in the bottom of my pack, gone by noon.

Would we do it again? The nine-year-old has already asked to go back "when I'm ten and can carry my own bear spray" (no), the six-year-old ranked it "better than the aquarium," and the toddler slept the entire drive home, which parents will recognize as a five-star review.
