Forty-one years of marriage and Gene still cannot read a trail map, which is how we ended up doing the Wawona Meadow Ramble backwards, but I'm getting ahead of myself.

We drove up from Fresno on a Sunday, the nineteenth of May, because our granddaughter Kaylee told us the dogwoods would be blooming and she was right, bless her. We are both in our seventies now — I'll be 74 in August, Gene turned 76 in March — so we don't do the big climbs anymore. Half Dome is for the young people and the foolish, and Gene says he was both once and has the knee to prove it.

The Wawona Meadow Ramble is what the ranger at the kiosk called "a gentle loop," maybe three and a half miles all told, though Gene's little watch said 3.8 by the end and Gene believes that watch the way he never believed me. The trail circles a wet meadow behind the old hotel, mostly flat, packed dirt and some boardwalk sections over the soggy parts. In May the soggy parts are very soggy. My shoes are still drying on the porch as I type this on the computer Kaylee set up for me.

Now, about going backwards. There's a sign at the trailhead suggesting you walk the loop clockwise so the morning sun stays out of your eyes. Gene looked at that sign, said "uh-huh," and marched off counterclockwise. So we squinted for the first hour. It didn't matter much. What mattered was the meadow itself, which was greener than anything we have at home, and the dogwoods, which were showing off shamelessly, and a coyote we watched mousing in the grass for a good ten minutes. He'd freeze, cock his head, pounce, miss, and look around like he was embarrassed. Gene said "I know the feeling" and I laughed so hard I had to sit down on a log.

We also saw a pileated woodpecker, or heard him first, hammering away like a man fixing a roof. Gene got a picture but his thumb is in it. There were mule deer at the far end of the loop, four of them, does, completely unbothered by us. And wildflowers I couldn't name, though a young woman with a field guide told me the purple ones were shooting stars. Imagine that. Flowers called shooting stars, just growing in the mud like it's nothing.

Conditions, for anyone planning: the trail was muddy in maybe a dozen spots, one stretch of a hundred feet or so where we had to pick our way along the edge on the grass. There was one tree down across the path near the halfway point, an easy step-over for Gene, a slightly less dignified maneuver for me. No snow anywhere at that elevation, though we could see plenty on the high country from the road. Bugs were only just starting; by June I expect that meadow will be a mosquito convention.

We took two and a half hours to do the loop, which included the coyote show, a sandwich stop, and Gene's mandatory nap-that-he-calls-resting-his-eyes on a bench near the golf course end. A younger couple passed us twice, which means they lapped us, and the second time the gal said "you two are adorable" and Gene said "she is, I'm just here to carry the sandwiches."

If you are our age, or your knees are, this is your trail. Bring waterproof shoes in spring, walk it clockwise like the sign says unless you're married to Gene, and don't hurry. The meadow rewards the slow. Everything does, we've found, but it took us forty years to learn it, and I'm giving it to you here for free.

We'll be back in the fall to see the colors, Lord willing. Kaylee says I should end these reports with a rating out of five stars like she does for restaurants. Fine. Five stars. One for each decade Gene has been getting us lost, minus one, plus one for the coyote.
