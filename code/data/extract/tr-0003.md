Let me start with the shuttle, because the shuttle is where the trouble starts.

You cannot drive into Zion Canyon. You park in Springdale, which costs money, then you stand in a line, then you stand in another line, then you ride a bus that is standing room only at 6:40 in the morning, packed shoulder to shoulder with people breathing their gas-station coffee on you, for half an hour. That's before you've hiked a single step. On September 6th, a Saturday, which yes, I know, was my first mistake.

We had permits for Angels Landing — you need a permit now, which you apply for in a lottery, which I lost twice before winning, so add "gambling" to the list of skills this hike requires. The permit checkpoint is at Scout Lookout, roughly two miles up, and the ranger there was, I will grudgingly admit, the most pleasant part of the morning.

The hike up to Scout Lookout is a series of paved switchbacks that the sadists who built this place named Walter's Wiggles, twenty-one of them, stacked on a cliff, and by number thirteen or so I had sweated entirely through my shirt. It was maybe 78 degrees at 8am and heading for the mid-nineties. Whoever rates this trail "strenuous" is a master of understatement; the total climb is something like fifteen hundred feet in a bit over two miles and most of it comes at you in two angry chunks.

Then the chains section. Half a mile along a sandstone fin with thousand-foot drops on both sides and a chain bolted to the rock to hold onto. Here's my complaint, and it's not the heights — I'm fine with heights. It's the people. The chain section is two-way traffic on a route that is emphatically one-person wide, so every forty feet you're negotiating right-of-way with strangers, some of whom are frozen with fear, some of whom are filming themselves, and one of whom was attempting the entire thing in Crocs while carrying an iced latte. A latte. On Angels Landing. The permit system was supposed to fix the crowding and I'd estimate it improved it from "unconscionable" to merely "bad."

The top. Fine. The top is spectacular. I'm not made of stone. You're standing on this island in the sky with the whole canyon unrolled beneath you, the Virgin River doing its lazy green loops fifteen hundred feet down, and even I shut up for about ten minutes. There are chipmunks up there with a bolder approach to armed robbery than anything I've seen in a city, and we watched a condor — an actual California condor, wingspan like a hang glider — ride the thermals below us. Below us. That part I have no complaints about, and believe me, I looked for some.

The descent was the ascent's problems in reverse order and higher temperatures. By the time we got down to the Grotto stop it was 96 degrees and the line for the shuttle back was forty minutes in full sun with no shade, because apparently shade structures are against somebody's principles. The trail itself, to be fair, was in perfect condition — dry, solid, well maintained, not so much as a loose bolt on the chains. The infrastructure around the trail is the problem. Also five miles round trip took us five hours, mostly spent waiting for other humans in various formations.

Would I do it again? Here's the thing that annoys me most of all: yes. Obviously yes. It's one of the great walks on the continent and everybody knows it, which is exactly why it's like this. Go on a Tuesday in November, bring more water than you think is legal, leave the latte at home, and budget an extra hour for standing in lines. And if you see a tall bald guy muttering about shuttle capacity, say hi, that's me, I'll be back next fall against my own better judgment.
