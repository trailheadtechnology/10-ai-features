The house got quiet in September when our youngest left for college, and Tom and I have been answering the quiet with trails ever since.

This is the fifth park in eight months, for those keeping score, which is nobody, which is the point. In April it was Yosemite, and on the 18th we hiked the Four Mile Trail from the valley floor up toward Glacier Point — and let me immediately address the name, because it lies. It is not four miles. It's closer to four point eight each way when the whole thing is open, and in mid-April it wasn't open the whole way; the top section was still closed for snow, so the route was however far you climb before the closure sign and your own judgment turn you around. For us that was a bit past Union Point, which I'd guess put our day around seven miles round trip and a good three thousand feet of climbing. "Four Mile Trail." Whoever named it also, presumably, described their fixer-upper as "cozy."

We started at 7:30 from the trailhead near Sentinel Beach, cool and perfect, maybe 48 degrees, the valley still in shadow while the top of El Capitan across the way was already lit up gold like a held-up trophy. The trail wastes no time — switchbacks from the start, endless oak-and-pine switchbacks with the valley floor dropping away below you at a rate that is either thrilling or alarming depending on which of us you asked. Tom counted the switchbacks for a while, lost count in the forties, and quietly retired from counting.

Here is what nobody tells you about hiking as a long-married couple: you find your separate paces and it's fine. Tom climbs like a diesel engine, slow and unstoppable. I climb in bursts with photo stops. We'd separate by a hundred yards and reconvene at the viewpoints, and every reconvening included him handing me exactly the snack I was about to want, which after thirty-one years is not telepathy, it's data.

And the views. This trail is a machine for manufacturing views of Yosemite Falls, which sits directly across the valley and in April — April, go in April — was at full spring thunder, all 2,400-some feet of it, upper fall, cascades, lower fall, the whole system pumping snowmelt and wearing a permanent scarf of mist. Every switchback reframes it. By Union Point you're nearly level with the top of the upper fall and you can watch the water leave the lip in slow-motion comets. We sat there for forty-five minutes with sandwiches and I cried a little, which I do at waterfalls now apparently, a menopause feature nobody documented.

Conditions report, since these write-ups ought to be useful: dry and well-groomed for the first two-thirds, then increasingly wet with snowmelt trickles crossing the trail, then patchy old snow in the shaded upper switchbacks, sun-cupped and slippery, right about where the closure signage told everyone to be sensible. Most people were. One young man in vans skated past the sign upward and we chose to believe in his guardian angels.

Wildlife: mule deer in the meadow at the bottom, acorn woodpeckers running their chaotic little stash-management operations in the oaks, a coyote trotting the road shoulder at dawn with somewhere to be, and — the headliner — two peregrine falcons doing display dives off the cliffs west of the falls, which a kind man with a scope let us watch. There's a couple even older than us, those falcons; they nest on these cliffs every year, he said.

Down took half the time up did and twice the toll on the knees; poles earned their keep. We were eating pizza in Curry Village by 3, planning the next park like a couple of kids planning a heist.

The quiet house started all this, but I want the record to show it isn't grief anymore. It's a travel budget. Rocky Mountain in July, if you're reading this, kiddo, and yes, you're invited — the guest room of this operation is always open.
