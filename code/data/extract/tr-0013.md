# The photograph I didn't get

Every landscape photographer has a shot list, and Avalanche Lake at first light has been at the top of mine for three years. Morning stillness, the cirque wall doubled in the water, waterfalls threading down through the reflection. I've studied other people's versions of it the way some men study box scores. On July 2nd I flew in with forty pounds of camera gear to finally make my own, and this is a report about not getting there, which turns out to be a story worth telling anyway.

I knew something was off when I checked the park's conditions page from the hotel in Whitefish the night before and saw Avalanche Lake Trail listed as closed. I'll be honest about my thought process: I assumed it was stale information, or an abundance of caution I could politely ignore, because I had come a long way and the human capacity for motivated reasoning is bottomless. So at 5:15am I was at the trailhead anyway, tripod strapped to my pack, walking into Trail of the Cedars in that blue pre-dawn light with the big cedars still holding the dark under them like water.

The closure is real. Where the lake trail leaves the boardwalk there's proper fencing and signage, and about a half mile up, past the stretch along the gorge, you reach the reason: the footbridge over Avalanche Creek is gone. Not damaged — gone. Two abutments and a span of churning teal water where a bridge used to be, a few splintered timbers wedged in the rocks fifty yards downstream. A trail crew member who arrived around 6:30 told me it washed out back in mid-June when the creek spiked with meltwater, June 18th or thereabouts, and that with the water still running this high there's no rebuild until it drops, so the lake is out of reach for the foreseeable — likely deep into July at the earliest, and that's not an official date, that's a guy in a hard hat squinting at a river.

Could a determined person ford it? The trail crew guy watched me think it and answered before I asked: people have tried, one got fished out cold and lucky last week, and it is a genuinely lethal piece of water right now. Looking at it, I believed him completely. The creek in that gorge doesn't flow so much as detonate continuously.

So: no lake, no shot list. What I had instead was a closed-trail dawn with almost nobody in it, and photographers are scavengers at heart, so I went to work with what the morning offered. The gorge itself, from the legal side of the fencing, is a subject I'd been treating as a consolation prize for three years and that's my mistake to own — that compressed rush of glacial water through red argillite, shot at a half-second exposure in low light, is as good as anything on my list. I made maybe sixty frames there. Then back into the cedars as the first real light came through, shooting straight up into the canopy, and a varied thrush started singing somewhere overhead, that eerie single buzzing note like a phone ringing in another room of the forest. A doe and fawn crossed the boardwalk forty feet ahead of me at 7:10 and I got three frames, one sharp.

By 8 the light went ordinary and the crowds arrived to be disappointed at the fence in shifts. I drank coffee from my thermos in the picnic area and watched a raven methodically unzip some unattended daypack pocket with the focus of a surgeon, and got that too.

Practical summary for anyone traveling far for this specific trail: it's closed, the bridge is fully out, don't build a trip around it until the park announces a reopening, and don't be the next person the trail crew fishes out. The cedars and the gorge remain open and are worth your morning on their own merits.

The lake will keep. That's the thing about a shot list — the good ones outlive your itinerary. I've rebooked for late September, when the water's low, the larches are turning, and there is, I'm told, a bridge in the budget.
